Ship repair work splits broadly into two very different operating environments, drydock and afloat, and the equipment each one calls for is genuinely different, not just a matter of scale. Understanding the distinction helps in scoping a repair project correctly from the start.

## What Is Drydock Repair?

Drydock repair takes place with the vessel fully removed from the water, typically in a purpose-built dock that's drained after the ship is positioned, exposing the entire hull for work. This gives full, unrestricted access to the hull below the waterline, which is essential for major structural repair, hull coating, or propeller and rudder work that simply can't be done with the vessel floating.

## What Is Afloat Repair?

Afloat repair happens with the vessel still in the water, either alongside a quay or at anchor, with work carried out from the deck, from the quayside, or from marine access equipment positioned against the hull. Afloat repair suits work that doesn't require full hull access below the waterline, such as deck equipment maintenance, upper hull coating and repair, or structural work above the waterline.

## Equipment for Full-Hull Drydock Access

Drydock repair typically draws on:

- **Scaffolding and fixed access structures** built around the exposed hull once the dock is drained
- **Boom lifts and aerial platforms** for working at height along tall vessel hulls, particularly for inspection, coating, and repair work on upper sections
- **Cranes**, for moving heavy components, equipment, or materials in and out of the dock area
- **Standard ground-based equipment generally**, since the dry dock floor provides stable, solid ground access once drained

## Equipment for Afloat, Barge-Based Access

Afloat repair requires equipment built for working against a floating or moving platform rather than solid ground:

- **Work barges**, providing a stable floating platform positioned alongside the vessel for crew and equipment access
- **Spider lifts**, compact tracked aerial platforms that can be mounted on a barge deck to reach hull sections above the waterline, where a standard boom lift's footprint and ground requirements wouldn't work
- **Marine-rated cranes and lifting equipment**, for moving materials and equipment between the quay, barge, and vessel

## Choosing the Right Approach for Your Repair Scope

- **Below-waterline work** (hull plating, propeller, rudder, underwater fittings) requires drydock access; there's no practical afloat alternative for this scope.
- **Above-waterline structural, coating, or deck work** can often be completed afloat, avoiding the cost and scheduling demands of a drydock booking.
- **Project timeline and drydock availability** matter too; drydock slots are a scarce, scheduled resource, and afloat repair is sometimes the practical choice purely due to drydock scheduling constraints, even for work that could technically go either way.

## FAQs

**Can all ship repair work be done afloat?**
No. Work requiring access below the waterline, such as hull plating or propeller repair, genuinely requires drydock access; afloat repair is limited to above-waterline and deck-level work.

**What's the advantage of afloat repair over drydock?**
Afloat repair avoids drydock scheduling and cost, and can often proceed without taking a vessel out of service as fully as a drydock period requires.

**Why use a spider lift instead of a standard boom lift for afloat work?**
Spider lifts are compact and trackable, allowing them to be mounted and operated safely on a barge deck, where a standard boom lift's footprint and ground stability requirements wouldn't be practical.

**How is equipment chosen for a ship repair project?**
Primarily by the actual repair scope, specifically whether below-waterline access is required, combined with drydock availability and project timeline considerations.
